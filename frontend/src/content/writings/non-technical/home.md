Humility and simplicity in almost everyone, except for the ones around my generation. People are generally not very rich; a lot of them are barely surviving on state benefits — whatever little of that actually makes it to them. They look like they gave up on their dreams long back, and many — to what should be a country’s collective horror — seem like they never even had one. They wear simple clothes, speak the local language, and always look like they’re supposed to be somewhere. They survive on hope. Hope that if they do the hard work and manage to put their kids through semi-proper schools, at least their kids can have a shot at the kind of life they never had. But part of me, the part that doesn’t really believe good exists without conditions, believes that could also be a way for them to secure a caretaker in old age, a shoulder to cry on, someone to buy medicines, someone to change their adult diapers, someone to set fire to their funeral pyre.

Sarju ([Kumaoni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumaoni_language): सरज्यू, [Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi_Language): सरयू) flows, and I’ve walked on its ‘ghats’ (riverbanks) ever since I was a kid. It is one of the spots where you can find the entire city making a trip to on weekends, if not to a temple. What used to be broken pavements and stairs have now been repaired, but only to be dirtied by people spitting here and there, knowing there won’t be consequences. Vendors have had their share in the waste too; since it’s one of the happening places in the city, how do you not expect the poor to sell some of the usual local traditional food and make a few bucks? Cows eat the leftovers, which is still acceptable, but then they also occasionally defecate there, which no public health or animal welfare authority seems to be concerned with.

But there is beauty in all this chaos too. Times have changed. You will see young girls coming out in groups, probably those returning from their evening tuition, which used to not be so common almost a decade ago. At least the country is starting to do better in this regard. Women dominate the vendor workforce too; from selling roasted corn to making tea in small sheds to selling sweets and samosas, everywhere you go you’ll see the true spirit of being the woman of the house. We have a long way to go, though, as a country to fully have a functional and equitable society for both males and females, but we shall get there.

Then there are small kids, running around, carefree. I don’t think they attend school if not for their midday meals. They seem mentally prepared to grow up to do what their parents have done, or in other words, take over the local businesses or farming their families have run. It might not be a bad thing, but personally I have always hated seeing potential wasted. If India was a bit better at tapping into potential, we could’ve had a great deal of talent come out from all sorts of places, and you never know, maybe the kid who is wasting away his time chasing the setting sun on the banks of Sarju could’ve been the next big thing. But as my parents keep reminding me, it is more important to be content on our own than to chase the setting sun and then live with the remorse of not being able to go back, having come this far in the chase of the impossible and yet being left with nothing but the dissatisfaction of not actually achieving what we wanted.

People talk politely. Humbleness comes from lesser education and forever thinking that they owe gratitude to those who are higher up in the social hierarchy, or maybe that is a pessimistic view and people are generally just nice in their personal capacity and have been taught great values. They still ride their old bicycles, contrasting hard with the new rich kids in their costly cars, which were probably gifts from their parents. What doesn’t seem to be going away is the culture of politics and idealization of illiterate politicians and people in power. The youth seem to be stuck on that front. It’s not bad to have a political opinion or practice your democratic rights, but the culture of politics in India (or for that matter anywhere in the world) is just pure shit. We could do better; there is just nothing good I can possibly write about this whole situation.

The city has survived with its culture and values for many years. With all its ups and downs, it is still the place that has raised me and made me and shaped me in many ways. It is a part of me, and I shall always be a part of it.

People here are very well-mannered, and they always smile at you and make you feel respected regardless of who you are. Sometimes it’s a bit difficult to understand them, and for them to understand me too, because I might have a very thick accent for them, but it works out well almost all the time. Small talk is apparently a thing, and it’s considered rude if you just mind your business. There is a certain sense of respect for one another, more than perhaps what I have seen back home. They value and respect one another not out of fear but out of their values for another human being, out of kindness for their own kind. They are considerate of others and seem very helpful. I, although, do not feel like talking to them because I do not like putting up a fake smile every time I have to talk to someone.

There are a lot of churches and beautiful architecture built around them. To my surprise, there are people always trying to give out free Bibles. I don’t think it’s evil to do that, but I am not a fan of people trying to get others to change their faith because there is a certain sense of superiority in it, if you look at it. Although the people seem harmless, they seem well trained to have surrendered to what has been fed to them, no different in concept than a jihadi who obeys his/her master without questioning, but perhaps (and to our relief) far less extreme than that. Everything is costly and the food is trash. I have not seen a lot of homeless people, but whatever little I have seen gives me the idea that survival is actually an issue here.

Evenings are very pretty; they have the skies that remind me of childhood. Pink, orange, and sometimes clear blue skies during the day remind me how long it has been since I saw something that real. It triggers some good dopamine inside my body. I go on walks, which started in the early evenings when the sun was about to set but have now extended to late nights. So far, it seems to be a safe place, with police patrolling every now and then, so late-night walks in solitude definitely seem very refreshing with loud music blasting.

But the best part of the walk has been the night sky lately. As a kid, I used to sleep outside whenever I visited my village, and they said that places with less pollution have visible night skies and the stars shine very bright. The oldest memory I happen to have of stars shining is from my village in my childhood. Urbanization here seems to have been kept in check very well, along with the amount of pollution that impacts the skies, because not only are the skies visible, but there is also room to actually see all of it at once without having to get on some rooftop. Temperatures go crazy low, or so I have heard. There have been cold days and there have been hot days, but neither of the extremes has been observed since I have been here.

There are very few people no matter where you go. This is something I have always wanted. If you want to know why, the answer is very simple — I hate people. I think this is how they came up with such beautiful and well-organized societies here. Everyone seems to have a good home, a small porch outside, a car, and more of those ordinary things just like in childhood movies, as I recall. Most people seem to be above average in terms of wealth compared to other places. Some I see are richer than others, but everyone seems to do something.

That something is very different from what these somethings were back home. People here are far more passionate about their trade than most. They do what they feel like doing. They look at the world and they seem to trust it. They believe that if they do what they like doing, then eventually they can be the best at it and make their living. Earning money does not seem to be the driving force of their lives; they want peace. Or maybe I am massively misjudging them and they are just lazy like the Europeans who have been accused of this for far too long now.

People follow traffic rules even late at night and stand and wait for the signal to turn green even on an empty road. I think this tells more about them than not. Either they are afraid of having to pay huge amounts of money in car repairs if they break traffic rules because they won’t get insurance (fear of law), or they simply have developed this habit of following the laid-down rules of engagement, be it in traffic with each other or with the law (well raised). Whatever the reason, people do have more respect for the laws here.

The footpaths are terrible. They are small and tilted — which was surprising to me considering that the place was built around a mountain — and in fall they had leaves all over them. But the worst is yet to come, SNOW! Starting December, I have been told that it will start snowing. It will be the first time I will be experiencing snow. I hope it stays a bit sunny and does not get gloomy. My room does not have windows, so it is mad depressing anyway, but I hope I will be pulling through it.

There is also huge diversity in the crowd; people of all faiths, regions, skin colors, ethnicities, and races can be seen. This has inherently helped me overcome my social anxiety a bit, because knowing that they are used to seeing guys who look like me means I won’t be an odd one out.

The houses are built like the churches. They have a certain European architecture here, and I have been told that historically it was occupied by Europeans (French and British), so it makes sense why it has a certain vibe to it, possibly Scottish. The trees in the beautiful fall weather have leaves ranging from yellow to pink to red on the same tree, and with a clear blue background it looks magical.

Like Sarju, two rivers — namely the Allegheny and Monongahela rivers — meet here at a junction point to form the Ohio River. Just like back there, here too people come to such places on weekends. The area where this junction point is located was a place where steel manufacturing used to take place, and now it has been converted into a tourist spot. The cafes are very small, and every place has its own menu and vibe. I already mentioned above that the food is trash, but it does not stop there; despite being trash, it is super unhealthy. The food that I get is also not fresh, and I am having to cook on my own, which has been a good ride so far, but I do not think I have it in me to do it all the time. After all, everything is far away, and I have since doubled my walking distance.

With all its ups and downs, I have forever dreamed of being here. It might not have fully sunk in that I am living the dreams of the young Abhinav. But at the same time, not-so-young Abhinav very well realizes that simply being at a place is of no good. Anyone can travel to any country provided they have the money, but what you do while you are at the place you always wanted to be decides what kind of life you will have.

I hope this new place is kind to me and that I am able to do justice to the dreams and responsibilities that I shoulder for my family, and above all, am able to build a living that is not serving, but rather feels like the warmth of home.
